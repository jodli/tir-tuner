# Where blood sugar comes from

Blood sugar is a balance. Sugar flows in from the liver and from food, and it flows out into the brain, the muscles and the fat tissue. The number on your sensor is whatever that balance leaves in the blood at the moment.

## The liver makes sugar all day

The liver stores sugar and releases it into the blood, day and night, whether you eat or not. That is how the brain stays fuelled while you sleep. Insulin is what slows the liver down: the more insulin in the blood, the less sugar the liver releases, and enough insulin stops it completely.

Without any insulin, the liver of a 70 kg adult releases about 13 g of sugar per hour. At 50 kg it is about 9 g. That is like a small snack every hour, without eating anything.

This is the job of basal insulin, the steady background insulin your pump delivers all the time. It keeps the liver in check. Meal insulin is a separate job.

## The brain takes sugar without asking

The brain and the red blood cells use sugar without needing insulin. They take a fairly steady amount, about 8 g per hour for a 70 kg adult, and that amount barely changes whether your sugar is normal or high. Only when sugar gets low does their uptake drop a little.

So without insulin the books don't balance: the liver puts in about 13 g per hour and the brain takes out about 8 g. The difference stays in the blood. That is why blood sugar climbs overnight in type 1 diabetes when basal insulin is missing, even without food.

## Muscle and fat need insulin

Most of the sugar from a meal ends up in muscle and fat tissue. These cells only take sugar in when insulin tells them to. Insulin moves sugar from the blood into the tissues, and then helps the tissues use it up or store it. Both steps depend on insulin, and both are covered on the [insulin page](insulin.md).

## The kidneys are a safety valve

The kidneys clean the blood and normally keep all the sugar in. Above about 160 mg/dL (9 mmol/L) they can't hold it back any more, and the extra sugar leaves with the urine. The higher the sugar, the more is lost.

This is why very high sugar often stops climbing by itself after a while, and why high sugar makes you thirsty and sends you to the toilet more often: the sugar takes water with it.

## Body size matters

Sugar spreads through the blood and the fluid around the cells. A smaller body has less of that fluid, so the same amount of sugar makes the level rise more. 30 g of carbs raises the sugar of a 50 kg person more than that of a 90 kg person, if everything else is the same.

## The resting point

If nobody eats and the basal insulin is just right, what the liver puts in equals what the body takes out, and the sugar stays flat. That is the goal of basal insulin: a flat line overnight and between meals. If the line climbs slowly in the night without food, there is too little basal insulin for that time. If it falls, there is too much.

Your body doesn't need the same amount all day. Hormones, sleep, activity and illness shift how strongly the body reacts to insulin, sometimes within hours. That is why a pump allows different basal rates and ratios for different times of the day.
